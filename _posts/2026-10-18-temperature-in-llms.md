Have you ever read a paper about large language models, or LLMs? If so, you have probably seen a value specified for a parameter called **temperature**.

Today, I want to explain what temperature in an LLM actually means.

I’ll start a little further back, with the algorithm through which I first encountered temperature as a parameter controlling probabilistic choices: **simulated annealing**.

## What is simulated annealing?

<p><a href="https://github.com/Tommaso-Vigano/simulated-annealing" style="display:inline-block;padding:0.7rem 1.1rem;border:1px solid #1f2937;border-radius:6px;background:#111827;color:#fff;font-weight:600;text-decoration:none;">View my C++ implementation on GitHub &rarr;</a></p>

Simulated annealing is a randomized algorithm that aims to find the global minimum of a function. It proposes neighbouring states and uses a temperature parameter to control whether it accepts a move to a worse state.

We are given a function $E(s)$, which computes the *energy* of a state $s$. Our goal is to find the state $s_{\mathrm{best}}$ at which $E(s)$ is minimized.

What role does temperature play?

The algorithm starts from a random state $s$. At each step, we choose a neighbouring state $s_{\mathrm{next}}$. If $E(s_{\mathrm{next}})<E(s)$, we accept it and update $s=s_{\mathrm{next}}$.

If you have studied calculus, you will know why accepting only improvements can be insufficient: we may get stuck at a local minimum. To help escape it, we sometimes accept a move even when $E(s_{\mathrm{next}})\geq E(s)$.

Writing $E=E(s)$ and $E_{\mathrm{next}}=E(s_{\mathrm{next}})$, we use the acceptance rule:

$$
P(E,E_{\mathrm{next}},T)=
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{True},&u\leq\exp\left[-\dfrac{E_{\mathrm{next}}-E}{T}\right],\\
\mathrm{False},&\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad T>0.
$$

Here, $u$ is a number drawn uniformly at random between $0$ and $1$. If the rule returns True, we move to the neighbouring state; otherwise, we stay where we are.

Let’s look at the role of temperature. Suppose the current state has energy $E=10$ and the proposed state has energy $E_{\mathrm{next}}=20$. The probability of accepting this worse state is:

$$
\exp\left[-\frac{20-10}{T}\right]=\exp\left(-\frac{10}{T}\right).
$$

For the three temperatures:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T=0.3&:\quad P(\text{accept})\approx3.3382\times10^{-15},\\
T=0.5&:\quad P(\text{accept})\approx2.0612\times10^{-9},\\
T=1&:\quad P(\text{accept})\approx4.5400\times10^{-5}.
\end{aligned}
$$

As temperature increases, so does the probability of accepting a worse state. A higher temperature therefore encourages **exploration**. During simulated annealing, the temperature is gradually reduced, making the algorithm more selective as the search progresses.

## How does this connect to temperature in an LLM?

Temperature plays a related role in an LLM: it controls how random the selection process is. Let’s look at the [official OpenAI documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/completions/methods/create), which describes a sampling temperature between $0$ and $2$ for its Completions endpoint:

> Higher values like 0.8 will make the output more random, while lower values like 0.2 will make it more focused and deterministic.

The idea sounds familiar, but let’s understand what this means for text generation.

An autoregressive LLM generates text by predicting the next token from the sequence of tokens it has already received or generated. At each step, it produces a probability distribution over possible next tokens.

For example, given the sequence “The kid is eating…”, the model might assign higher probabilities to food-related continuations such as “chocolate,” “ice cream,” or “a hamburger,” and lower probabilities to continuations such as “a bell” or “a nation.” These words illustrate the idea; a word can consist of more than one token.

The model first produces raw scores called **logits**. These are numbers that can be positive or negative. They are not probabilities: they do not have to lie between $0$ and $1$ or add up to $1$.

To turn them into probabilities, we use a function called **softmax**.

![A two-token softmax example showing probabilities between zero and one that sum to one](/assets/images/llm-temperature-softmax.png)

*A simple example with two tokens: one has logit $z$ and the other has logit $0$. Their probabilities always sum to $1$.*

The standard softmax formula is:

$$
\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{z})_i
=\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j}},
$$

where $z_i$ is the logit for token $i$ and $K$ is the number of possible tokens.

Temperature changes the equation slightly. For $T>0$:

$$
P_i(T)=\frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j/T}}.
$$

When $T$ is small, dividing by it increases the differences between logits. Exponentiation amplifies those differences, so the token with the highest logit receives a larger share of the probability. Sampling becomes more concentrated on the most likely choices, making the output less varied.

With a higher temperature, the differences between the scaled logits become smaller. The probabilities become more evenly distributed, giving less likely tokens a greater chance of being selected and increasing randomness.

At $T=1$, we recover standard softmax. A temperature setting of $0$ is usually handled by selecting the highest-scoring token directly, rather than dividing by zero.
