I recently came across *On Brevity*, an ironic one-page paper by Seb Krier.

Behind the joke lies a serious argument. Now that producing large quantities of text has become easy, we should reconsider how we associate length with effort and quality since a shorter and clearer text may have required considerably more thought.

This argument is particularly relevant in the age of generative AI.

Generative AI can produce grammatically correct paragraphs almost instantly. It can expand a simple idea into an essay, transform a few bullet points into a report or produce several pages around a relatively small amount of information.

Consequently, we are increasingly exposed to writing that is fluent but repetitive: I'm pretty sure you can detect most of the LinkedIn posts written with the AI since they are identical.

This changes the value of some traditional signals. In the past, the length of a document could suggest that considerable time and effort had been invested in it. Today, length is no longer reliable evidence of either since generating more text is often the easiest option.


## But is this really new?

At this point, however, I started to question whether this conclusion is specific to generative AI.

Has it not always been more desirable—and considerably more difficult—to produce a short, clear text rather than a long and unnecessarily complex one?

Writing at length allows us to include every thought as it appears. Concise writing requires to understand the subject well enough to distinguish what is essential from what is merely additional. We must organise the text and remove useless repetition.

From this perspective, generative AI has not created the value of brevity, it just increased it.

## Pascal’s observation

This idea is confirmed by the historical reference mentioned in Krier’s paper.

In the [sixteenth of his *Lettres provinciales*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ABlaise_Pascal_-_Les_Provinciales.djvu/148), Blaise Pascal wrote:

> Je n’ai fait celle-ci plus longue que parce que je n’ai pas eu le loisir de la faire plus courte.

This can be translated as:

> I made this one longer only because I did not have the leisure to make it shorter.

Pascal expressed essentially the same idea centuries before generative AI: writing more is often the quicker option, while writing less requires time, selection and revision.

His letter was not longer because length was more desirable but only because he did not have enough time to make it concise.

The familiar principle “less is more” summarises this idea well. It does not mean that every text should be reduced to the smallest possible number of words but instead it means removing what does not contribute, so that what remains can communicate more effectively.
