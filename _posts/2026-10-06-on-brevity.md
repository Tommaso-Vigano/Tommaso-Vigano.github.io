![The title and abstract of On Brevity by Seb Krier](/assets/images/on-brevity.png)

I recently came across *On Brevity*, an ironic one-page paper by Seb Krier. Its introduction consists of only three words: “See abstract.”

Behind the joke is a serious argument. Now that producing large quantities of text has become really easy, we should reconsider how we associate length with effort and quality. A long document may appear thorough while containing little useful information. A short, clear and high-signal text may have required much more thought.

Generative AI makes this especially relevant. Producing grammatically correct paragraphs is now almost effortless. As a result, we are increasingly exposed to text that is fluent but repetitive: many words, few decisions and little information.

But is this problem really new?

## Brevity before generative AI

Long before language models, writers understood that concise communication was difficult.

In the sixteenth of his *Lettres provinciales*, Blaise Pascal wrote:

> Je n’ai fait celle-ci plus longue que parce que je n’ai pas eu le loisir de la faire plus courte.

This can be translated as:

> I made this one longer only because I did not have the leisure to make it shorter.

Pascal’s observation reveals an important asymmetry. Writing more is often the quickest option. Writing less requires identifying the essential argument, removing repetition and deciding what the reader genuinely needs.

Generative AI did not create this principle. It amplified it.

## Length is not effort

We often use visible length as a proxy for invisible effort. A long report appears more substantial than a short one. A detailed answer looks more carefully researched than a concise response.

That heuristic becomes unreliable when text can be generated almost instantly.

The relevant question is no longer:

> How much was written?

It is:

> How much useful information was preserved?

Editing a text down requires judgement. Every removed sentence creates a decision: was it redundant, or did it contain necessary context? Good brevity is therefore not simply deletion. It is compression without destroying meaning.

## Shorter is not always better

The goal should not be to make every text as short as possible. Some ideas require context, qualifications and examples. Removing too much can produce writing that is elegant but incomplete.

The best text is not necessarily the shortest one. It is the shortest text that still accomplishes its purpose.

Brevity should increase information density, not eliminate nuance.

## A scarce resource

Generative AI has made words abundant, but it has not made attention abundant. The reader’s time remains limited.

This changes the responsibility of the writer. Producing a first draft may now be cheap, but selecting, organising and compressing its ideas remains difficult. The most valuable part of writing is increasingly not generation, but judgement.

Perhaps this is the lesson behind *On Brevity*: concise writing does not indicate a lack of effort.

It often demonstrates where the effort went.
